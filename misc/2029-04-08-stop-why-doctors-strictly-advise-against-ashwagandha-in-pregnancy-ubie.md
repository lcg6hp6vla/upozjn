Why Your Doctor Strictly Advises Against Ashwagandha During Pregnancy

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Ashwagandha is not recommended during pregnancy. There are no reliable human studies confirming safe dosing, and animal research suggests it may cause uterine contractions, miscarriage, fetal growth delays, and hormonal imbalances. The FDA and ACOG advise against its use, and prenatal care providers recommend safer, evidence-based alternatives for stress, sleep, and immune support.

Key risks include early labor, developmental issues, and disrupted hormone balance. Safer options exist for managing pregnancy-related stress and fatigue.

Why Your Doctor Strictly Advises Against Ashwagandha During Pregnancy

Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) is an adaptogenic herb prized in Ayurvedic medicine for its stress-reduction, energy-boosting, and immune-supporting properties. As more pregnant people explore natural remedies, questions arise about "Ashwagandha and pregnancy safety." Despite its popularity, medical experts strongly caution against using ashwagandha while pregnant. Below, we summarize the key reasons—based on credible research and guidelines—so you can make an informed choice and discuss any concerns with your healthcare provider.

1. Limited Human Data on Pregnancy

Most clinical trials of ashwagandha have been conducted in healthy adults, not pregnant people.

No large, well-designed studies have established safe dosing, timing, or long-term effects on fetal development.

Without solid human data, doctors cannot predict how ashwagandha may affect uterine activity, hormone balance, or fetal growth.

2. Animal Studies Raise Red Flags

Animal research provides early warning signals about potential risks:

Uterine Stimulation & MiscarriageSome rodent studies show that Withania extracts can increase uterine contractions, raising the theoretical risk of preterm labor or miscarriage.

Fetal Growth & DevelopmentHigh doses in pregnant rats have been linked to lower birth weights and delayed developmental markers in pups.

Hormonal ChangesAlterations in estrogen and progesterone levels were observed, which may disrupt the hormonal environment critical for a healthy pregnancy.

While animal studies don't always translate directly to humans, they highlight possible dangers when human trials are absent.

3. Potential Mechanisms of Risk

Several active compounds in ashwagandha may influence pregnancy:

Withanolides: Bioactive steroids that could modulate the immune system and reproductive hormones.

Thyroid Stimulating Effects: Ashwagandha can increase thyroid hormone levels, which may complicate pre-existing thyroid disorders (e.g., hyperthyroidism) and impact fetal brain development.

4. Official Guidelines and Recommendations

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) classifies many herbal supplements as "unchecked" due to lack of rigorous safety data.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) advises caution with any supplement that lacks clear evidence of safety in pregnancy.

The National Institutes of Health (NIH) notes that "there is not enough reliable information to know if ashwagandha is safe to use when pregnant."

Given this, most prenatal care providers err on the side of avoiding ashwagandha until after childbirth and breastfeeding.

5. Common Concerns among Pregnant People

Many expectant parents consider ashwagandha for:

Stress, anxiety, and sleep support

Fatigue and low energy

Immune health

However, safer, well-studied alternatives exist:

Stress & Anxiety: Prenatal yoga, mindfulness, cognitive-behavioral techniques

Sleep Trouble: Gentle sleep hygiene, warm baths, magnesium under medical supervision

Fatigue: Balanced diet, hydration, gentle exercise, iron/folate checks

Immune Support: Adequate sleep, hand hygiene, prenatal vitamins

6. Possible Side Effects of Ashwagandha

Even outside pregnancy, ashwagandha may cause:

Upset stomach, diarrhea, nausea

Headache or dizziness

Allergic reactions (rare)

Hormonal shifts affecting thyroid and adrenal function

During pregnancy, side effects like gastrointestinal upset or thyroid changes could lead to dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, or uncontrolled thyroid levels—which in turn could stress both mother and baby.

The supplement industry is not tightly regulated:

Product labels may be inaccurate or misleading

8. When to Seek Professional Help

If you're pregnant and experiencing serious or life-threatening symptoms—such as heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, or signs of preterm labor—please speak to a doctor immediately or call emergency services. For non-urgent concerns like stress, fatigue, or mild joint pain, consult your prenatal care provider before starting any new herb or supplement.

10. After Delivery and During Breastfeeding

Breastfeeding safety data is also limited—active compounds may pass into breast milk.

Discuss timing with your doctor; they may recommend waiting until you've established breastfeeding and assessed your baby's reactions.

Start with a low dose under professional guidance if you choose to introduce it post-partum.
