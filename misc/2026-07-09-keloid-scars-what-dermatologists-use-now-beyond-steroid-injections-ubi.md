Keloid Scars: What Dermatologists Use Now Beyond Steroid Injections

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Modern keloid treatment combines multiple therapies for better results. Beyond steroid injections, dermatologists now use silicone gel sheets, cryotherapy, laser treatments, intralesional chemotherapy, and surgical excision paired with post-operative radiotherapy or pressure therapy. Newer agents like botulinum toxin and verapamil are increasingly added to flatten scars and prevent recurrence.

Choosing the right combination depends on scar size, location, skin type, and prior treatment response. Because keloids vary widely and recurrence is common, identifying your specific symptoms early is key to selecting the most effective therapy. Explanation

Keloid scars form when your skin overreacts to injury, producing too much collagen and creating a raised, firm scar that can keep growing beyond the original wound. While steroid injections have long been a mainstay, dermatologists now combine multiple approaches to improve outcomes and reduce recurrence. Below is a practical overview of current keloid scar treatment options, explained in clear language and organized for easy reading.

Understanding Keloid Scars

Keloids differ from regular or hypertrophic scars in that they:

Extend beyond the boundaries of the original injury

Tend to grow over months and rarely regress on their own

May cause itchiness, tenderness or cosmetic concern

Risk factors include darker skin tones, family history, certain body sites (chest, shoulders, earlobes), and injuries such as piercings, acne or surgical incisions.

Why Beyond Steroid Injections?

Intralesional corticosteroids (e.g., triamcinolone) work by reducing inflammation and collagen production. However:

Up to 50% of keloids recur after steroid alone

High doses can thin the skin, cause pigmentation changes or atrophy

Some keloids respond poorly or plateau in size reduction

To boost effectiveness, dermatologists layer or combine therapies—known as multimodal treatment.

Current Keloid Scar Treatment Options

1. Silicone Gel Sheets and Silicone Gel

Silicone is one of the safest, first-line adjuncts.

Creates a hydrated environment that regulates collagen

Easy to apply under clothing or dressings

Typical use: 12-24 hours daily for 2–6 months

Low risk of side effects; skin irritation in a minority

2. Cryotherapy

Freezing with liquid nitrogen causes controlled tissue damage:

Best for smaller keloids or earlobe keloids

Often combined with steroids or intralesional 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)

Side effects: blistering, pigment changes

3. Laser Therapy

Lasers can target blood vessels (reducing redness) and remodel collagen. Common types include:

Pulsed-dye laser (PDL) at 585–595 nm

Fractional CO₂ or erbium lasers to ablate scar tissue

Often combined with topical or intralesional drugs for deeper penetration

4. Intralesional Chemotherapy

Injecting drugs directly into the scar:

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU): inhibits fibroblast proliferation

Bleomycin: cytotoxic to scar tissue, used in resistant keloids

Often alternated or combined with steroids for synergy

Side effects: pain at injection site, ulceration in high doses

5. Surgical Excision Plus Adjuvant Therapy

Surgery alone carries a high recurrence rate (up to 80%), so dermatologists routinely add:

Immediate post-op radiotherapy (low-dose)

Steroid injections or 5-FU starting within 24–48 hours of excision

Pressure therapy or silicone sheets during healing

6. Radiation Therapy

Low-dose superficial X-ray or electron beam:

Administered within 24–48 hours post-surgery

Reduces fibroblast activity

Requires specialized equipment; minor risk of skin changes

7. Pressure Therapy

Custom pressure earrings or garments:

Used mainly for ear keloids or after ear reconstruction

Applies constant pressure (20–30 mmHg) to flatten scars

Worn 12–24 hours daily for several months

8. Topical and Intralesional Novel Agents

Emerging and off-label options under study:

Verapamil (calcium-channel blocker): reduces fibroblast proliferation

Botulinum toxin (Botox): relaxes surrounding muscle tension, may alter scar formation

Imiquimod cream (immune modulator): applied after excision to reduce recurrence

Onion extract gels: mixed evidence, but low risk

9. Microneedling and Laser-Assisted Drug Delivery

Microneedles or lasers create microchannels enabling deeper penetration of:

Steroids

5-FU

Other topical anti-scarring agents

This approach can improve efficacy while using lower drug doses.

10. Combination Protocols

Most successful protocols use at least two modalities. An example regimen:

Surgical removal

Immediate radiotherapy

Monthly steroid/5-FU injections for 3–6 months

Silicone sheet application daily

At-Home Care and Maintenance

Even with in-office procedures, ongoing self-care matters:

Gently massage scars with silicone gel or a moisturizing cream

When to "Speak to a Doctor"

While keloids are benign (non-cancerous), you should seek prompt medical attention if you experience:

Rapid growth, bleeding or severe pain

Always discuss any new or worsening symptoms with a healthcare provider. If you suspect life-threatening complications or feel ill in other ways, contact medical services immediately.
