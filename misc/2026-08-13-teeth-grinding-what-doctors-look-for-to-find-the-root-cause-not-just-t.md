Teeth Grinding (Bruxism): Finding the Root Cause, Not Just Treating the Symptom

Teeth grinding (bruxism) affects up to 20% of adults at some point. While a night guard or stress ball may ease jaw pain, doctors dig deeper to uncover what's truly driving the problem. Here's how healthcare professionals go beyond treating the teeth-grinding symptom to get to the root cause.

What Is Bruxism?

Bruxism is the medical term for clenching or grinding your teeth. It can occur:

During sleep (sleep bruxism)

While awake (awake bruxism)

Left unaddressed, it can lead to:

Worn or cracked teeth

Jaw pain, stiffness or popping (TMJ issues)

Headaches, earaches or neck pain

Sleep disturbances for you and your partner

Why Doctors Look Beyond the Symptom

Simply wearing a night guard treats the damage, not the driver. To achieve lasting relief, a doctor or dentist will look for:

Underlying medical conditions

Mental health factors

Lifestyle habits

Medication side effects

By pinpointing the cause, you'll get more targeted treatment—and better long-term results.

1. Detailed Medical and Dental History

Your doctor starts with questions such as:

When did you first notice teeth grinding bruxism?

Do you wake up with headaches or jaw soreness?

Have you had recent dental work or orthodontic treatment?

Are you diagnosed with sleep apnea, acid reflux or chronic headaches?

What medications or supplements are you taking?

Knowing your full health picture helps providers spot patterns or risk factors.

2. Physical and Dental Examination

In-office checks include:

Dental wear patterns: Flattened, chipped or worn enamel can point to chronic bruxism.

Jaw joint (TMJ) exam: Feeling for clicking, popping or restricted movement.

Muscle palpation: Pressing on the masseter and temporalis muscles to find tender "trigger points."

Bite assessment: Looking for misaligned teeth (malocclusion) that may contribute to clenching.

Your dentist may take digital scans or X-rays to rule out fractures, cavities or joint damage.

3. Sleep Studies and Monitoring

If sleep bruxism is suspected, a sleep specialist may recommend:

Polysomnography (PSG): A comprehensive overnight study measuring brain waves, breathing, oxygen levels, heart rate and muscle activity.

Home sleep tests: Simpler devices that track breathing and muscle activity.

Portable EMG (electromyography): Sensors on facial muscles record grinding episodes.

These tests can also screen for sleep apnea—a common driver of night-time teeth grinding.

4. Psychological and Stress Assessment

Stress, anxiety or mood disorders often trigger awake bruxism. Your doctor may:

Ask about work, relationship or financial stressors

Screen for anxiety, depression or PTSD

Recommend brief questionnaires (for example, the Perceived Stress Scale)

Refer you to a mental health professional if needed

Addressing stress through therapy, relaxation techniques or biofeedback can drastically reduce grinding.

5. Lifestyle and Habit Evaluation

Certain behaviors can worsen teeth grinding bruxism:

Caffeine and energy drinks

Alcohol and recreational drugs

Smoking or vaping

Chewing gum or pens

Poor sleep habits (irregular schedules, screen time before bed)

Your care plan may include cutting back on stimulants, improving sleep hygiene and replacing chewing habits with healthier alternatives.

6. Medication Review

Some prescription drugs list bruxism as a side effect:

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), like fluoxetine

Other antidepressants or antipsychotics

Certain stimulants (used for ADHD)

Never stop or change a prescription without talking to your doctor. They may adjust the dose or switch medications to see if grinding improves.

7. Putting It All Together: Individualized Treatment Plans

Once the root cause is identified, your healthcare team can tailor treatment:

• For stress-related bruxism – Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) – Relaxation exercises (deep breathing, meditation) – Biofeedback devices – Night guard as a temporary protective measure

• For sleep apnea–related bruxism – Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy – Oral appliances to keep airway open – Weight management and positional therapy

• For bite-related bruxism – Orthodontic correction (braces or aligners) – Occlusal adjustment (reshaping tooth surfaces) – Fixed or removable dental splints

8. Simple Self-Care Tips

While you're working with your care team, try these steps at home:

Practice jaw relaxation: Place your tongue between teeth to train a "rest" position.

Massage your cheeks and temples for 2-3 minutes, twice daily.

Apply a warm compress to the jaw area before bed.

Stick to a calming bedtime routine—limit screens 1 hour before sleep.

Keep a diary of grinding episodes, stress levels and diet.

These habits support professional treatments and speed up relief.

9. When to Seek Help

If you notice any of the following, see a healthcare professional:

Severe or persistent jaw pain

Cracked, loose or shifting teeth

Chronic headaches or earaches

Loud grinding that disrupts your or your partner's sleep

Signs of sleep apnea (loud snoring, gasping for air at night)

10. Next Steps and Talking to Your Doctor

Discovering the root cause of teeth grinding bruxism takes collaboration between you, your dentist and possibly other specialists (sleep medicine, psychology, ENT). Start by:

Gathering your medical and dental records

Noting when and how often you grind

Identifying stressors or lifestyle factors

Scheduling an evaluation with your dentist or primary care doctor

Using tools like a symptom diary or the free symptom checker above

Remember: if you experience life-threatening symptoms—such as severe breathing difficulties, uncontrolled pain or unexplained weight loss—speak to a doctor right away. Early intervention can prevent long-term damage and improve your quality of life.
