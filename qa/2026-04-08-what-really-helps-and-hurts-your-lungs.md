# What Really Helps and Hurts Your Lungs

YOUR LUNGS POWER a workout, filter out invaders, and work for you 24/7—unless germs, pollutants, bad luck, or our own neglect gets in the way. Take a deep breath and discover how to help them out.

What They Do

YOU KNOW FROM high school that your lungs bring in oxygen and move it to the blood (inhale) while taking carbon dioxide out of the blood (exhale). Not as well-known: Your lungs are also “immunologically the biggest filter ever,” says Ana Zamora Martinez, M.D., a critical-care pulmonologist at the Mayo Clinic. Their branched structure’s tiny passages for blood and air naturally restrict pollution, germs, and even blood clots. And the lungs help regulate blood pressure by processing a hormone called angiotensin that’s created in the kidneys.

How They Do It All

THERE ARE ROUGHLY 8.4 million passageways (aka airways) a breath can travel through to get from your trachea to your bloodstream. The smallest is less than a millimeter in diameter. After air goes in your trachea, it travels through your bronchi to your lungs, then passes into progressively smaller tubes called bronchioles. It eventually reaches alveoli, super-small air sacs surrounded by capillaries. That’s where the key handoff happens: Oxygen diffuses into the blood and CO2 diffuses out. The surface area of all of the alveoli is roughly the size of a tennis court and allows tons of oxygen to be exchanged quickly. That’s especially handy when sprinting for a plane or across a finish line.

What Can Go Wrong

ASTHMA is a “hypersensitivity” or constriction of the small airways that makes breathing difficult, says Jess Mandel, M.D., the chief of the Division of Pulmonary, Critical Care, and Sleep Medicine at UC San Diego Health. It’s most common in childhood, but there’s a lesser-known second peak of onset in your 40s and 50s for reasons that aren’t fully understood yet. There’s no cure for the condition, just smart management. That largely means avoiding triggers, which vary for everyone, but the most common are the things you’d expect: smoke, mold, air pollution, infections. Treatments have improved: Inhalers can help open constricted airways, and medications treat the underlying inflammation that causes the problem. If you cough and wheeze only after strenuous physical activity, you might have a related phenomenon called exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. Formerly known as exercise-induced asthma, it may be caused by irritation from dry air. (The air we breathe through our mouth is drier than what we breathe through our nose.) It can be managed—likely with an inhaler—and you can still work out.

RESPIRATORY INFECTIONS like pneumonia and colds come from your environment. “The good news is that the surface area of a lung is enormous and it’s open to the outside world—but that’s also the bad news,” says Dr. Mandel. Here’s where the lessons of Covid (itself a respiratory illness) come in: Masking and proper hygiene can help prevent these infections.

SMOKING-RELATED DISEASES, caused primarily by cigarette smoking (weed and vapes are bad, too), can destroy alveoli and coat your airways with tar and inflammatory debris. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a biggie—the third leading cause of death worldwide.

LUNG CANCER is the leading cause of cancer death, far outstripping prostate cancer in men. Treatment usually includes a mix of surgery, radiation, and chemo.

Lose Extra Weight

Anything that restricts the lungs’ ability to expand will make it harder to breathe—and that includes extra fat on your chest wall.

Workout

Regular exercise makes your heart and lungs stronger, more efficient machines, and it helps muscles learn to use oxygen more effectively. Aim for at least the national guideline of 30 minutes, five days per week, and keep in mind that both endurance and strength work benefit the lungs.

Check Air Quality

Your lungs are great at trapping bad stuff before it gets to your bloodstream, but some particles from wildfires and gas-burning vehicles are so small that they slip right by the alveoli and may go on to damage your brain, arteries, and heart. Help your lungs out: If the AQI is rising, opt for a short and hard workout over a long and low-impact one. On really bad air days, exercise indoors.
