Vulva Pain? Why It's Hurting & Medically Approved Next Steps

Pain in the vulva can be uncomfortable, frustrating, and sometimes scary. The good news is that most causes of vulva pain are treatable. The key is understanding what might be going on and knowing when to seek medical care.

The vulva includes the outer parts of the female genitals — the labia (inner and outer lips), clitoris, vaginal opening, and surrounding skin. Because this area has many nerve endings and delicate skin, it's especially sensitive to irritation, infection, and hormonal changes.

Below, you'll find common reasons for vulva pain, what symptoms to watch for, and medically recommended next steps.

Common Causes of Vulva Pain

1. Irritation or Contact Dermatitis

One of the most common causes of vulva pain is simple irritation.

The vulva is sensitive to:

Scented soaps or body washes

Bubble baths

Laundry detergents

Tight clothing or synthetic underwear

Pads or panty liners

Feminine hygiene sprays

Symptoms may include:

Burning

Stinging

Redness

Mild swelling

Itching

Next steps:Switch to fragrance-free products, wear breathable cotton underwear, and avoid overwashing. The vulva does not need harsh cleansing — warm water is often enough.

2. Yeast Infections

A vaginal yeast infection can cause pain that extends to the vulva.

Common symptoms include:

Intense itching

Thick white discharge (often described as cottage cheese–like)

Pain during urination or sex

Yeast infections are common and usually treated with antifungal medication (over-the-counter or prescription).

If it's your first infection or symptoms are severe, speak to a doctor to confirm the diagnosis.

3. Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)

While BV primarily affects the vagina, the vulva can feel irritated or sore.

Thin gray or white discharge

Fishy odor

Mild burning

BV requires prescription antibiotics. It does not usually cause severe vulva pain, so if pain is intense, another cause should be considered.

4. Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs)

Certain STIs can cause vulva pain, especially if sores or lesions are present.

Examples include:

Genital herpes – painful blisters or ulcers

Chlamydia or gonorrhea – may cause irritation or discharge

Syphilis – painless sores in early stages

If you notice:

Blisters

Open sores

Unusual discharge

Pain during urination

You should seek medical testing promptly. Early treatment prevents complications.

5. Vulvodynia

Vulvodynia is chronic vulva pain lasting at least three months without a clear cause. It is a recognized medical condition.

Pain may feel like:

Rawness

Throbbing

Pain during sex or tampon use

The vulva may look completely normal.

Treatment options include:

Topical medications

Nerve pain medications

Pelvic floor physical therapy

Counseling if pain impacts emotional wellbeing

If pain has lasted for months and testing hasn't found infection or skin disease, ask a doctor about vulvodynia.

6. Bartholin's Cyst or Abscess

The Bartholin's glands sit on either side of the vaginal opening. If blocked, they can form a cyst.

Symptoms include:

Swelling on one side of the vulva

Tenderness

Pain when sitting or walking

Fever (if infected)

If the cyst becomes infected, it can turn into an abscess and become very painful.

7. Skin Conditions Affecting the Vulva

The vulva can develop skin conditions just like any other part of the body.

Lichen sclerosus – thin, white, fragile skin

Lichen planus – inflammation causing soreness or discharge

Psoriasis or eczema

Persistent itching

Pain

Skin color changes

Cracking or bleeding

These conditions require medical evaluation and often prescription creams.

8. Hormonal Changes

Low estrogen levels — especially during menopause, breastfeeding, or certain medical treatments — can cause vulva dryness and thinning.

This may lead to:

Pain during sex

Irritation

Increased sensitivity

Topical estrogen therapy is often very effective and medically approved when appropriate.

9. Trauma or Injury

Vulva pain can also result from:

Friction during sex

Bicycle riding

Childbirth

Accidental injury

In most cases, symptoms improve with rest and avoiding further irritation.

When Vulva Pain Could Be Serious

Most vulva pain is not life-threatening. However, seek urgent medical care if you have:

Severe swelling and fever

Rapidly worsening pain

Spreading redness

Signs of allergic reaction (difficulty breathing, facial swelling)

A painful lump that's getting bigger

Unexplained bleeding

A sore that doesn't heal

Rarely, persistent changes in the vulva — especially lumps, skin thickening, or non-healing sores — can signal vulvar cancer. While uncommon, early evaluation is important.

If something feels wrong or unusual for you, trust that instinct and speak to a doctor.

Bottom Line

Vulva pain is common and usually treatable. Causes range from simple irritation to infections, cysts, skin conditions, or chronic pain syndromes.

Pay attention to:

How long the pain lasts

Whether there is swelling, discharge, or sores

If pain is mild, moderate, or severe

Any fever or systemic symptoms
