# You Actually Burn Calories While Sleeping. Doctors Explain How.

SLEEPING IS A time to rest and replenish, and you know that getting plenty of shut-eye is good for you in many ways. Sleep gives you energy and keeps your body working well.

You probably don’t think of sleep as a time to burn calories, though. But the truth is your body is always burning some, no matter what you're doing—and that includes being sprawled out across your bed sleeping.

“The body is always burning calories,” explains Alex Dimitriu, M.D., a double-board certified physician in psychiatry and sleep medicine and founder of Menlo Park Psychiatry and Sleep Medicine.

Even during total inactivity or sleep, the body’s basal metabolic rate (BMR) reflects the number of calories your body needs at rest to carry out basic functions like breathing, circulation, digestion, and cellular repair, Dr. Dimitriu says.

Your BMR is “essentially the minimum energy required for essential physiological functions keeping us alive,” explains Alex Rothstein, C.S.C.S., coordinator and instructor for the exercise science program at the New York Institute of Technology.

While you will burn some calories as you sleep, it’s minimal, to be clear. And there are several factors that come into play that influence that number, including how much you weigh, how active you are, and how well you’re sleeping.

Your sleeping calorie burn won’t rival your latest HIIT or powerlifting workout, but it will add to it—as long as you’re sleeping the recommended minimum of seven hours a night, something most people don’t do. Here’s what you should know about burning calories while sleeping.

How many calories do you burn while sleeping?

TYPICALLY, YOU’LL BURN 40 to 55 calories per hour of sleep, according to Harvard Medical School. But that can add up. Sleep seven hours a night, and it equals 280 to 385 calories.

That can vary from person to person, however. Some people have higher BMRs than others. Exercise and building muscle can raise it, while weight gain can lower it, according to Harvard. Medical conditions and medications might affect it, too.

Generally, the body burns about 15 percent fewer calories when you’re asleep versus being awake, Dr. Dimitriu says. You’ll even burn more calories while lounging around than sleeping, since you’re moving around some and using muscles.

Can you increase your calorie burn during sleep?

ANYTHING THAT INCREASES your BMR also increases the number of calories you’ll burn while sleeping, Rothstein says.

Having more muscle mass increases “around-the-clock calorie burn,” Dr. Dimitriu explains. Your physical activity level and the foods you eat also affect BMR, Rothstein says, adding that a regular workout routine will improve your sleep overall.

Getting at least seven hours of sleep a night and good quality sleep will also improve the number of calories that you burn overnight. Here are some tips for improving sleep hygiene, according to the National Sleep Foundation:

Set a schedule: Go to bed and wake up at the same time each day, even on weekends.

Establish a bedtime routine: Read a book, meditate, or listen to relaxing music to wind down.

Avoid screens: Turn off noisy TVs and avoid scrolling through your phone before dozing off, which might keep you up.

Create a calming space: Make sure your bedroom is quiet and comfortable. A room temperature of 67 degrees Fahrenheit is ideal for sleeping. Make sure the room is dark and that your bedding is soft and cozy.

Skip alcohol and caffeine: These substances can disrupt your sleep, so avoid them a few hours before bedtime.

Don’t eat before bed: Finish your meals and snacks about three hours before bedtime; otherwise, it could disrupt your sleep.

Make sure you treat any health or sleep conditions. Dr. Dimitriu says some people with sleep apnea may technically burn more calories while asleep, as they exert more effort to breathe, but the end result is sleep disruption, which often leads to weight gain and health problems.

“People with sleep disruption for any reason ranging from poor bedtime habits to shift work to sleep apnea will all have negative impacts on their metabolism,” he explains. This may be evident during the day when you’re craving high-carb foods and constantly feeling hungry.

Sleep Affects Weight Beyond Just Nighttime Calorie Burn

WHEN IT COMES to weight and sleep, it's not just about how many calories you burn as you snooze. There are other important ways that sleep affects your weight. When it comes to keeping the pounds in check, both the quality and quantity of your sleep are important.

A constant lack of Zs wreaks havoc on your hunger hormones, upping the amount of the appetite-stimulating ghrelin and decreasing the appetite-suppressing leptin in the body.

Good sleep boosts daytime metabolism, controls appetite, and improves the quality of foods that you’re likely to choose, Dr. Dimitriu says. People who don’t get enough deep sleep are often hungrier during the day and may choose more calorie-dense food.

Use of glucose, or the fuel your body uses for energy, is highest when you’re awake, lowest during deep sleep, and intermediate during REM sleep, which is the phase when you dream, he explains.

Sleep affects other weight-regulating hormones as well. “Sleep deprivation, even short term, can result in insulin and glucose imbalances,” says Kuljeet Gill, M.D., a sleep medicine specialist at Northwestern Medicine. Lack of sleep can also affect how balanced cortisol and your thyroid hormones are.

“While it is true that you can burn more calories being awake, the impact of getting sufficient sleep is much larger,” Dr. Dimitriu says. “Insufficient sleep time has been linked to obesity as well as diabetes due to the metabolic disturbance that results from poor or insufficient sleep.”

A good night’s rest is critical to all physiological functions, and not getting the right amount can be detrimental. It can even affect your workouts.

Sleep “influences everything from how you perceive your workout, how tired it is making you, or how focused you are on form, to how much endurance and strength you have,” explains Rothstein. “It then influences how well you recover from the workout in order to adapt and come back stronger and more resilient.”
