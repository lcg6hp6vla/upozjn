# Is Scarcity Brain the Reason You're Miserable?

ALMOST ANY BEHAVIOR is fine in moderation. But why do we suck so bad at moderating? Why do we keep eating when we’re full? Why do we keep shopping when we own too much? Why do we keep drinking when we’re already tipsy? Why do we scroll social media when it makes us miserable? Why do we binge-watch another episode even when we realize a more meaningful life beyond the screen is passing us by? Why do we get stuck doing the same thing we regret over and over and over?

[Reason You're Miserable](https://brainmessenger.xianfengchem.com/blog/6676382668.html)

These behaviors are often fun and rewarding in the short term but backfire in the long run. In researching my new book, I learned that these behaviors track back to how we react to feelings of “scarcity.” All it takes is a “scarcity cue” to get there.

A scarcity cue is a piece of information that fires on our scarcity mindset. It leads us to believe we don’t have enough. We then instinctually fixate on attaining or doing that one thing we think will solve our problem and make us feel whole.

Scarcity Brain: Fix Your Craving Mindset and Rewire Your Habits to Thrive with Enough

Scarcity cues are like air: all around us and inside us. They can hit us through advertising, social media, news, chats with coworkers, walks in the neighborhood, and so much more. They can be direct and all-encompassing, like a sagging economy or a global pandemic. Or they can be subtle and slight, like our neighbor buying a shiny new car.

Our reaction to scarcity isn’t anything new. It’s an ancient behavior system that evolved naturally in the human mind to help our ancestors survive. Scientists detailed our scarcity mindset and reaction to scarcity cues as early as 1795. The topic is now an intense area of research for psychologists, anthropologists, neuroscientists, sociologists, economists, and biologists. Today it’s well accepted that for most of human history, obeying the next scarcity cue and constantly craving and consuming more kept us alive. We evolved in harsh environments that had one thing in common: They were worlds of less, of scarcity.

Food, information, influence, stuff, time on earth, what we could do to feel good, were scarce, hard to find, and short-lived. The people who survived and passed on their genes chased more. They defaulted to overeating, amassing stuff and information, seeking influence over others and their environments, and pursuing pleasure and survival drives to excess.

Obeying this evolutionary drive for more still makes sense for all species. Except one. As humans figured out how to make things faster and cheaper during the Industrial Revolution, our environments of scarcity rapidly shifted to those of plenty. By the 1970s, the benefits of this revolution had spread to most people in developed countries. They’ve been rippling out across the globe ever since.

We now have an abundance—some might say an overload—of the things we evolved to crave. Things like food (especially the salty, fatty, sugary variety), possessions (homes filled with Amazon Prime purchases), information (the Internet), mood adjusters (drugs and entertainment), and influence (social media).

Yet we’re still programmed to think and act as if we didn’t have enough. As if we’re still in times of scarcity. That three-pound bundle of nerves in our skull is always scanning the background, picking up and prioritizing scarcity cues, and pushing us to consume more.

We’re still compelled to eat more food than we need. To impulsively search for more information. To buy more unnecessary stuff. To jockey for more influence over others. To do what we can to get a fleeting hit of pleasure. To fixate on getting what we don’t have rather than using and enjoying what we do have. We have a scarcity brain.

The science shows that our scarcity brain doesn’t always make sense in our modern world of abundance. It now often works against us, and outside forces are exploiting it to influence our decisions. It’s at the root of the counterproductive behaviors we can’t seem to shake, the habits that put a hard brake on improving our physical and mental health, happiness, and ability to reach our full potential.

Aren’t addiction, obesity, anxiety, chronic disease, debt, environmental destruction, political dispute, war, and other scourges driven by our craving for more?

Humanity has experienced big scarcity cues before. But the Covid-19 pandemic occurred at a particularly inopportune or opportune moment—depending on your vantage: a time when technology had accelerated to deliver abundant access to everything we’re built to crave while also giving corporations unprecedented insight into exactly how they can leverage our scarcity brain to bend our behavior. Especially those behaviors we can repeat over and over and over in rapid succession—eventually to our detriment. It’s as if there’s some larger behavior pattern at play, a scarcity loop.

Slot machines, sports gambling, dating apps, TikTok, chasing “likes” on social media—they have the three parts of a scarcity loop: opportunity, unpredictable rewards, quick repeatability. The opportunity to get something of perceived value starts it off. Not knowing exactly when we’ll get that thing of value sucks us in—that’s unpredictable rewards. We experience an exciting, suspenseful anxiety as we wait to see whether this occasion will deliver the good stuff. Research shows that our brains fixate on uncertainty. They naturally suppress systems that take in other information, and we focus on whatever is unpredictable. The loop aspect is that you can quickly repeat the behavior, and you can repeat it infinitely. As with TikTok or dating apps, you swipe, swipe, swipe until you get what you want. Then repeat swipe, swipe, swipe, boom. All kinds of algorithms learn to breadcrumb users.

It seems the scarcity loop is the serial killer of moderation.

5 Tactics to Escape the Scarcity Loop

Crush Food Cravings

Easter traveled to Bolivia to meet the Tsimane, a healthy group of hunter-gatherers who eat the same nutrient-dense food most meals every day. They grow, hunt, and cook their own food and eat plates loaded with corn or rice plus a bit of fish, chicken, or meat, and some vegetables. Fill up like them.

Reimagine Your Motivation

Be vigilant about self-conscious emotions, stuff like shame, jealousy, and pride that can depend on other people’s feelings. There are two kinds of pride, authentic (what you feel when you do awesome things) and hubristic (when you falsely advertise yourself). Emphasize authentic pride.

End Death-Scrolling

Resist your informavore brain and its craving for certainty. The unpredictable rewards of information we read online don’t always improve our lives or lead to understanding and often stress us out. Instead, exercise your drive to explore; break new ground on the map and in your mind.

Resist the Lure to Gamify

Easter calls activity trackers “babysitters” and says by leaning into the process of exercise rather than fixating on steps, you can reap greater rewards. More broadly, we can determine what we want the rewards of a behavior to be. They don’t have to be likes, follows, grades, rankings, or scores.
