# The 'Peformative Male' is All Over the Dating Apps. Here's What it Means to be One IRL.

IT'S THE MODERN mating ritual: Guys strategically sprinkle green flags through their dating app profiles like bait for women looking for long-term, emotionally-available partners. A therapy mention here, a sunrise hike there, maybe a perfectly tousled pic with someone’s niece to signal “great with kids” (yet always clarified with “not my kid” in his bio). For the most part, he’s done the impossible–presented himself as a one of the hard-to-find good guys. And if he seems too good to be true, he probably is.

Rather than acknowledging that he’s a real guy—with all the faults and foibles inherent to being human—this type of dude is a “performative male.” The term describes a man who adopts certain personas and interests to project emotional intelligence, cultural awareness, and a sophisticated kind of strength, not because he is those things, but because he knows it’ll score him points with the opposite sex. There’s the rugged outdoorsy guy who journals after morning yoga, the fit and sensitive guy who quotes Rupi Kaur under a gym selfie, and the minimalist feminist who proudly supports women in between cold plunges.

Regardless of the form in which he manifests, the performative male cares less about genuine personal growth and more about optics. “It’s masculinity as performance; it’s an identity built for approval instead of authenticity,” says Kyler Shumway, PsyD, a licensed clinical psychologist and founder/CEO of Time for Therapy. In many ways, he’s not just putting on a performance for women; he’s performing as the kind of man women have been taught to want. Case in point: The brooding, yet emotionally literate, love interest straight out of a BookTok romance or an episode of The Summer I Turned Pretty (yes, we're talking about you, Connie Baby).

This particular brand of masculinity has been carefully curated for female consumption, and it's working... for now. “You can trick someone into liking you at first, but eventually, the real you comes out,” says New York-based matchmaker and dating expert Anna Morgenstern. “The goal [of dating] is to show off your authentic self—your interests, hobbies, and passions. Sharing personal stories and your vision for the future is how you build intimacy.”

The Rise of the Performative Male

THE CONCEPT OF performing in dating is nothing new. We’ve all added an inch or two to the height listed in our dating app bio (guilty), or claimed to “love the outdoors” when, in reality, we’d rather be anywhere but a campsite. The performative male persona, however, is a new addition to the cultural zeitgeist—so new that Google searches for the term only started spiking in July, the same month Urban Dictionary added it to their lexicon of slang.

It’s become such a phenomenon that performative male contests (they’re satire–mostly) have popped up across the country in recent months. “This is a mandolin I bought to learn how to play. I never learned how to play it, but it’s a conversation starter,” said one contestant at a San Francisco event in August. To complete his ensemble, he carried Michelle Obama’s memoir, Becoming (purchased two days prior), all while walking a dog and clutching a canvas tote–neither of which were his.

And now with Halloween in full swing, social media meme accounts are offering up the performative male as a convenient costume idea, while poking fun at the type of man who will actually wear it out. One such post from @overheardnewyork classifies this person as “definitely a Brooklyn-based creative slash DJ slash photographer who's 'in on the joke' [because] like...how come you had all this stuff at home in the first place?” In just a few short months, the internet has turned the cultural phenomenon of the performative male aesthetic into a full-blown parody.

But for many men, embodying the qualities of a performative male isn’t a joke; it’s a survival tactic. “Masculinity itself isn’t the problem. The problem is the narrow script men are handed about what it means to be a man,” says Shumway. “At some point, acting confident or controlled helped him survive something hard and got him praise, attention, or safety.”

Now, instead of those traits helping men take down a wooly mammoth, they're being used to rake in likes. “Social media platforms are designed to reward content made for attention, not authenticity,” says Shumway. “The more extreme and engagement-worthy the content, the more it gets promoted by the algorithm. That’s why performative versions of masculinity spread so quickly.” He points out that much of this stems from influencer culture—think fitness creators, lifestyle gurus, and self-proclaimed “alpha men” who equate masculinity with power and status.

TikTok and AI have only accelerated the trend, says Morgenstern: “Men can now hire professionals to rewrite their profiles or use tools like Chat GPT to choose photos and optimize their bios—all in the hopes of attracting more matches.”

Recent data from Hinge may further explain the origins of the performative male. The popular dating app reported a 217-percent spike in Google searches for the word “chalant” (as in, the opposite of nonchalant), reflecting what they believe to be a growing “desire for effort” when looking for a partner. Similarly, 72 percent of women surveyed by Hinge in August 2025 said they cared more about a potential partner putting effort into building a relationship than having a high income.

Still, the research showed a striking disconnect between what men think women want versus what they actually value. Men guessed body attractiveness (24 percent), facial attractiveness (21 percent), and financial wealth (20 percent) would be at the top of women’s lists; meanwhile, women rated emotional availability (35 percent), acknowledgement and respect for emotional needs (25 percent), and consistent communication (22 percent) as most desirable traits in a partner.

In response to modern daters’ desires, the performative male typically acts in one of two ways: Either he flexes his social status, physical strength, or financial worth—because he assumes that’s what women want—or he showcases emotional depth and vulnerability to appeal to what women actually want, but in a way that’s calculated rather than authentic.

What Qualifies as Performative Male Behavior

“PERFORMATIVE MASCULINITY CAN show up in small habits or full-blown personas,” says Shumway. That’s what makes this behavior tricky to spot, even if you’re the one doing it.

Why Being a Performative Male is a Problem

SURE, BEING A performative male might get you a few extra right swipes and some short-term validation, but you can’t just “fake it till you make it.” Eventually, reality will catch up with you because the performance isn’t sustainable. Plus, you're doing your real self a disservice. “You can’t change or grow when you’re always trying to look strong,” says Sumway. “Most of the time, change starts when the performance stops working and the relationship falls apart.”

Being a performative male not only hinders personal growth, but it also hurts the person they're dating. “While the intent might not be malicious, authenticity often comes into question. Instead of connecting with someone who shares their genuine interests, these men are casting a wide net to increase their match count, and women are the ones who ultimately suffer,” says Morgenstern. “Women are already skeptical of dating apps, and performative male behavior only makes it harder to trust the process. After a string of disappointing matches, it’s easy to start doubting yourself.”

The performative male isn’t immune to self-doubt, either. Not only is it one of the driving forces behind performative manhood—a guy believes his authentic self isn’t “enough,” so he pretends to be someone else—but it can also drive a couple apart. No matter how much a partner tries to validate or connect with the other person, they can’t fill the internal void. “The truth is that you can love someone deeply and still be starved for closeness when the relationship revolves around an identity that’s just for show,” says Shumway.

How to Move From Performance Toward Authenticity

IT'S TOTALLY POSSIBLE to ditch the performative male persona; you just can’t shy away from self-reflection.“Notice the moments when you’re performing and ask yourself why,” says Shumway. “Who are you trying to impress? What would happen if you stopped? From there, it’s about learning to trust who you are, even when you’re not being impressive.”

Therapy can be a powerful starting point to unpack the roots of the performance, but it’s not the only option. “Therapy helps, but so do real friendships where honesty is valued more than image,” Shumway says. “Most of us imagine vulnerability to mean sharing our deepest fears or most painful experiences, but vulnerability is really just about sharing our experiences with others.”

And no, that doesn’t mean relying on a close female friend or family member to be your sounding board. You can practice being vulnerable anywhere with anyone. In fact, it may be as simple as expressing yourself in real time, says Shumway. “It’s about helping others understand your internal world. Sharing things as small as ‘I love this song’ or ‘I don’t like spiders’ may not feel important, but they help others see a bit more of the real internal you.”

Shumway wishes there were more environments where men could safely practice being real, but group therapy remains one of the few spaces built for honesty over image. “It’s one of the best places to develop comfort with vulnerability,” he says. Look for local groups in your area, and think of it like hitting the emotional gym. “The more you practice being real with safe people, the easier it becomes everywhere else,” he says. “Authenticity is a muscle that gets stronger with use.”
