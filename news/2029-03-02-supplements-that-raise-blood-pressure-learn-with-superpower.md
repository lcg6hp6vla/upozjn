What these supplements actually do to blood pressure

The supplements that raise blood pressure don't all work the same way. Some mimic hormones that control fluid balance. Others stimulate the sympathetic nervous system, the branch responsible for the fight-or-flight response. A few interfere with how blood pressure medications are metabolized. Understanding the mechanism matters because it determines who's at risk, which medications interact, and how long the effect lasts.

Licorice root and aldosterone mimicry

Licorice root contains glycyrrhizin, which is metabolized to glycyrrhetic acid. This compound inhibits the enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2), which normally inactivates cortisol in the kidneys. When inhibited, cortisol accumulates and activates mineralocorticoid receptors, causing sodium and water retention while depleting potassium. The result is a rise in blood pressure that mimics primary hyperaldosteronism. The effect persists as long as you're consuming licorice and can take weeks to reverse after stopping.

Caffeine and sympathetic activation

Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, which normally act as a brake on the sympathetic nervous system. Without this inhibitory signal, sympathetic outflow increases, raising heart rate, cardiac output, and peripheral vascular resistance, all of which elevate blood pressure. A single dose of 200–300 mg can raise systolic blood pressure by 8–10 mmHg for up to five hours. The effect is most pronounced in people who don't regularly consume caffeine. Habitual coffee drinkers develop tolerance, so their blood pressure response is blunted. But high doses, especially from supplements or energy drinks, can still raise blood pressure even in regular users.

St. John's wort and drug metabolism

St. John's wort (Hypericum perforatum) is a potent inducer of cytochrome P450 enzymes, particularly CYP3A4, and P-glycoprotein. Some antihypertensive medications, particularly calcium channel blockers such as amlodipine, nifedipine, and verapamil, are metabolized by CYP3A4. By accelerating their metabolism, St. John's wort can reduce plasma concentrations of these drugs, diminishing their blood pressure-lowering effect. The interaction risk is high enough that people taking antihypertensive medications should avoid this supplement entirely.

Bitter orange and synephrine

Bitter orange (Citrus aurantium) contains synephrine, an adrenergic amine with structural similarity to epinephrine. Synephrine activates alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, causing vasoconstriction and increased heart rate, which raises blood pressure. The effect is dose-dependent and amplified when combined with caffeine, a common pairing in weight loss supplements.

Yohimbe and alpha-2 receptor blockade

Yohimbe contains yohimbine, which blocks alpha-2 adrenergic receptors. These receptors normally provide negative feedback that limits norepinephrine release; by blocking them, yohimbine increases circulating norepinephrine. Yohimbe also causes peripheral vasoconstriction, further elevating blood pressure. The effect is consistent and can be severe in people with existing hypertension.

Asian ginseng and dose-dependent effects

Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng) contains ginsenosides that can have bidirectional effects on blood pressure depending on dose and specific ginsenoside composition. At lower doses, some ginsenosides increase sympathetic activity and raise blood pressure; at higher doses, others promote nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation and lower it. Meta-analyses show no consistent effect overall, but individual responses vary. People with hypertension should approach ginseng cautiously, especially at lower doses.

How blood pressure regulation responds to these supplements

Blood pressure is controlled by multiple overlapping systems: the sympathetic nervous system, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, endothelial function, and fluid balance. Supplements that raise blood pressure disrupt one or more of these pathways.

Sympathetic nervous system activation

Caffeine, bitter orange, and yohimbe all increase sympathetic nervous system activity. This raises heart rate, increases cardiac output, and constricts peripheral blood vessels. The result is higher blood pressure. The effect is immediate and dose-dependent. In people with hypertension, baseline sympathetic tone is already elevated, so adding a stimulant supplement can push blood pressure into dangerous territory.

Fluid and electrolyte balance

Licorice root disrupts the kidney's ability to regulate sodium and potassium. Sodium retention increases blood volume, which raises blood pressure. Potassium loss can cause muscle weakness, arrhythmias, and further blood pressure elevation. This mechanism is slower than sympathetic activation but more sustained. It takes days to weeks to develop and just as long to reverse.

Endothelial function and nitric oxide

Some supplements, including high-dose ginseng, may improve endothelial function and increase nitric oxide production, which dilates blood vessels and lowers blood pressure. But at lower doses, ginseng can have the opposite effect. The dose-response relationship is not linear, and individual variation is high.

Drug metabolism and medication efficacy

St. John's wort doesn't directly raise blood pressure in most people. Instead, it reduces the effectiveness of blood pressure medications by speeding up their metabolism. The result is the same: blood pressure rises. This interaction is particularly dangerous because it's invisible. You're taking your medication as prescribed, but it's not working as well as it should.

What the evidence says about blood pressure effects

The strength of evidence varies by supplement. Some have robust clinical data. Others rely on case reports and mechanistic plausibility.

Licorice root

A 2024 randomized crossover trial found that even a low daily intake of 100 mg glycyrrhizic acid increased systolic home blood pressure by an average of 3.1 mmHg and suppressed renin and aldosterone by 30% and 45% respectively. Larger doses used in traditional supplements can raise blood pressure by 10–15 mmHg or more. The effect is dose-dependent and reversible. Licorice is found in candies, teas, and herbal supplements, often without clear labeling of glycyrrhizin content.

Caffeine

Meta-analyses consistently confirm that caffeine raises blood pressure acutely by 3–8 mmHg systolic and 4–6 mmHg diastolic, with larger effects in non-habitual consumers. The rise lasts up to five hours. However, very high doses, especially in people with severe hypertension, can double the risk of cardiovascular events.

St. John's wort

St. John's wort does not directly raise blood pressure but reduces the efficacy of antihypertensive medications through CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein induction. Case reports document breakthrough hypertension in patients whose blood pressure was previously controlled on antihypertensives after starting St. John's wort. Clinical interaction databases classify this as a major drug interaction.

Bitter orange

Case reports and small clinical studies link bitter orange and synephrine-containing products to acute hypertensive episodes and cardiovascular events. The FDA issued warnings about synephrine-containing products marketed as ephedra replacements. The effect is amplified when combined with caffeine, a common formulation in weight loss products.

Yohimbe

Clinical studies show yohimbine raises systolic blood pressure and heart rate in a dose-dependent manner. Adverse event reports to the FDA include hypertensive crisis and tachycardia linked to yohimbe-containing products. Yohimbe is not recommended for people with hypertension or cardiovascular disease.

Asian ginseng

Evidence for ginseng's blood pressure effects is mixed. Some RCTs show modest increases at low doses, while others show modest decreases at higher doses. A systematic review found no statistically significant overall effect on resting blood pressure. Given this inconsistency, people with hypertension should use caution and monitor blood pressure when starting ginseng.

Who should avoid these supplements

Not everyone responds to these supplements the same way. Baseline blood pressure, medication use, genetics, and dose all influence the response.

People with existing hypertension

If your blood pressure is already elevated, adding a supplement that raises it further increases your risk of stroke, heart attack, and kidney damage. Licorice root, yohimbe, and bitter orange should be avoided entirely. Caffeine should be limited, especially from concentrated sources like energy drinks or pre-workout supplements.

People taking blood pressure medications

St. John's wort can reduce the effectiveness of calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, and other antihypertensives. Caffeine and stimulant supplements can counteract the effects of blood pressure medications. If you're on antihypertensive therapy, check with your doctor before starting any new supplement.

People with cardiovascular disease

Yohimbe and bitter orange increase heart rate and blood pressure, which can strain the heart. People with coronary artery disease, heart failure, or arrhythmias should avoid these supplements.

Pregnant women

Licorice root consumption during pregnancy has been associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. A Finnish cohort study published in the American Journal of Epidemiology found that children born to mothers with high glycyrrhizin intake (≥500 mg/week) scored 7 IQ points lower and had 3.3-fold higher odds of ADHD problems compared to children of low-exposure mothers. Pregnant women should avoid licorice-containing supplements and foods.

People with kidney disease

Licorice root disrupts sodium and potassium balance, which can worsen kidney function and fluid retention in people with chronic kidney disease.

How to use this information to make smarter supplement decisions

Knowing your baseline blood pressure and tracking changes over time is more informative than guessing whether a supplement is affecting you. Blood pressure varies throughout the day, so single readings don't tell the full story. Home monitoring, ideally at the same time each day, provides better data.

If you're considering a supplement that can raise blood pressure, check your blood pressure before starting and monitor it regularly for the first few weeks. If your systolic blood pressure increases by more than 10 mmHg or your diastolic by more than 5 mmHg, the supplement may be the cause.

Related biomarkers can add context. Potassium levels drop with licorice use. Elevated cortisol can indicate excessive licorice consumption. Tracking high-sensitivity C-reactive protein can help assess whether blood pressure changes are accompanied by increased cardiovascular inflammation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What supplements raise blood pressure the most?

Licorice root and yohimbe have the most consistent and significant blood pressure-raising effects. Licorice works by mimicking aldosterone and causing sodium retention, while yohimbe blocks alpha-2 receptors and increases norepinephrine release. Both can raise blood pressure substantially, especially in people with existing hypertension.

However, very high doses from supplements or energy drinks can raise blood pressure even in habitual users, and people with severe hypertension may be at increased cardiovascular risk.

Is it safe to take St. John's wort if I have normal blood pressure?

St. John's wort rarely raises blood pressure directly in people with normal baseline levels. The bigger concern is drug interactions. It induces liver enzymes that metabolize many medications, including blood pressure drugs, antidepressants, and birth control pills. If you're taking any prescription medications, check with your doctor before using St. John's wort.

How much licorice root is safe if I have high blood pressure?

If you have hypertension, it's best to avoid licorice root entirely. Even small amounts, as low as 100 milligrams of glycyrrhizin per day, can raise blood pressure in susceptible individuals. Licorice is found in candies, teas, and herbal supplements, often without clear labeling, so avoidance is the safest approach.

Can ginseng lower blood pressure instead of raising it?

Ginseng has a dose-dependent effect on blood pressure. Low doses tend to increase blood pressure, while high doses may lower it through nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation. The overall effect is inconsistent across studies, and individual responses vary. People with hypertension should use ginseng cautiously and monitor their blood pressure closely.

How long does licorice root's blood pressure effect last after stopping?

The blood pressure elevation from licorice root persists as long as you consume it and can take several weeks to reverse after stopping, because the underlying aldosterone-like sodium retention resolves gradually. Glycyrrhizin metabolizes to glycyrrhetic acid, which inhibits 11β-HSD2 and allows cortisol to chronically activate mineralocorticoid receptors in the kidney — the resulting sodium retention and potassium depletion are not acutely reversible. The potassium depletion also compounds cardiovascular risk independently of blood pressure.

Can high-dose ginseng raise or lower blood pressure?

Ginseng has bidirectional blood pressure effects that depend on dose, preparation, and the individual's baseline cardiovascular status — it may raise blood pressure in some contexts and lower it in others. Ginsenosides activate multiple receptor pathways simultaneously: some promote nitric oxide production (vasodilatory) while others stimulate sympathetic activity (vasoconstrictive), and the net effect varies by ginsenoside composition between Asian and American ginseng. Clinical trials show inconsistent results, so disclose ginseng use to your provider if you are monitoring blood pressure or on medication.
